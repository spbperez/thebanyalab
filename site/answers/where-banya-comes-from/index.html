Where does banya come from?

From the Slavic village bathhouse — for most of its history the cleanest and hottest room a family had, and the one they used for everything that mattered.

Written accounts of bathhouses in Slavic lands run back to the medieval chronicles, and the practice is older than the writing about it. For centuries a banya was a small log building at the edge of a household, heated by a stove packed with stones.

The oldest form is the black banya, built without a chimney. The fire burns, smoke fills the room and blackens the walls, and when the wood is spent the smoke is let out while the heat stays in the stone. Chimneys came later. Practitioners still argue about which gives better air.

It was never only about washing. In village life the banya was the cleanest heated space a family had, so it was where women gave birth, where the sick were brought, and where the dead were prepared. A thousand years of a culture's most serious moments happened in that room. That is the weight the word carries, and it is why we do not call this a spa.

The venik came out of the same practicality. Birch and oak grow everywhere in those forests. The branches are cut in early summer while the leaf is still soft, dried through the year, and soaked back to life in hot water before a session.
